Title: LEGAL DEPARTMENTS: Lean, Mean, Legal Machines | Asian Legal Business
Fetched: 2026-08-25T16:51:02.281Z

[Skip to main content](https://www.legalbusinessonline.com/features/legal-departments-lean-mean-legal-machines#main-content)

![](https://www.legalbusinessonline.com/sites/default/files/inline-images/shutterstock_1807321420.jpg)

The ALB & Multilaw In-house Counsel Survey 2025 reveals Asia's legal departments juggling strategic advisory, cost management, and technology adoption with remarkably lean teams. For in-house counsel, this means punching above their weight as trusted business partners navigating budget cuts, AI adoption, and cross-border complexity.

When 116 in-house lawyers across Asia were asked to describe their biggest challenge, the answers painted a stark picture: Rising workloads, shrinking budgets, and teams stretched thinner than ever. Welcome to the reality of corporate legal departments in 2025.

The ALB & Multilaw In-house Counsel Survey 2025 reveals a profession operating at a fascinating crossroads. These lawyers have become strategic advisers, cost managers, technology pioneers, and talent recruiters all rolled into one. And they're doing it with remarkably small teams: nearly half work in departments of just one to five people, yet they're supporting operations that span continents.

The numbers tell a compelling story about resilience under pressure. While 40 percent of respondents reported moderate budget cuts or hiring freezes and another 14 percent faced significant cuts, these lean teams continue to punch above their weight. Nearly half engage with executive leadership weekly or more, cementing their role as strategic partners rather than mere service providers.

But this influence comes at a cost. When asked about their top departmental challenges, 53 percent cited rising workloads without additional headcount, while 52 percent pointed to budget pressures. The classic squeeze: do more, with less, and do it faster.

### The Triage Imperative

Faced with this reality, legal departments are making hard choices about where to focus their energy. The survey reveals a clear hierarchy of cost-management strategies, with prioritising high-risk matters only emerging as the overwhelming favourite. More than half of respondents chose this as their top approach, accounting for 26 percent of all selections across ranking positions.

This triage mentality represents a fundamental shift in how legal teams operate. Rather than trying to be all things to all people, they're drawing clear lines about what deserves lawyer attention and what doesn't. Self-service portals, template libraries, and automated workflows handle the routine stuff, freeing lawyers to focus on the matters that could genuinely hurt the business.

Bringing work in-house ranked second among cost-control strategies, representing 23 percent of all selections, with roughly 31 percent selecting it as their primary approach. The data shows just how far this trend has gone: 61 percent of departments already handle 80 percent or more of their legal work internally, directing less than a fifth of their budget to outside counsel.

This shift is changing what in-house teams expect from external partners. "In-house teams are not just looking for technical capability; they want trusted partners who can extend their reach, align with business priorities, and deliver predictable value," says Adam Cooke, CEO of Multilaw. The survey data backs this up: when choosing external counsel, respondents ranked cost efficiency and flexible pricing as the most important factor, alongside sector-specific expertise, responsiveness and the ability to coordinate cross-border matters seamlessly.

Technology and automation ranked third at 21 percent of all selections, chosen by about 14 percent as their top strategy. What's striking is how much faith legal leaders are placing in these tools, particularly generative AI, to help them escape the resource trap. Half of respondents described their legal-tech adoption as moderate, with significant room for improvement, while another 32 percent are in early-stage pilots.

### TOP PRIORITY FOR NEXT 12 MONTHS

Supporting business in cross‑border growth was the most frequently ranked No. 1 priority, chosen by 38 percent of respondents. Talent retention and upskilling ranked first among 18 percent, while reducing external legal spend and implementing or expanding legal tech both drew about 15 percent. Strengthening data protection and cybersecurity followed closely with 14 percent.

![](https://www.legalbusinessonline.com/sites/default/files/inline-images/top%20priority.png)

The enthusiasm for AI is palpable: 66 percent are either using or exploring generative AI tools, making it the most widely adopted technology by far. Contract lifecycle management systems came in second at 49 percent, followed by legal research automation at 41 percent. One respondent captured the sentiment well: "A strong team with a solid legal foundation will never be overtaken by AI but can only grow with new technology."

Yet this optimism is tempered by caution. When asked about concerns, 73 percent cited quality and reliability of outputs, while 72 percent highlighted data security and confidentiality risks. These lawyers aren't resisting change; they're pragmatists who understand that deploying AI without proper guardrails could create more problems than it solves. Only 34 percent feel very confident in their organisation's ability to respond to a major data breach, underscoring the need for robust governance frameworks before scaling AI deployment.

The upskilling priorities reflect this dual challenge. Generative AI and legal innovation topped the list at 59 percent, closely followed by technology and legal automation at 55 percent, and cross-border regulatory knowledge at 54 percent. Legal departments recognise they must build capabilities for tomorrow while managing today's demands.

### The Talent Puzzle

If budget pressures are the immediate crisis, talent retention may be the longer-term threat. The survey reveals a troubling pattern: 55 percent rated their ability to attract and retain top legal talent as good but with occasional gaps, while 28 percent described it as outright challenging.

The reasons for turnover tell a story about the human cost of doing more with less. Lack of career progression topped the list at 46 percent, followed by uncompetitive compensation at 28 percent. Unmanageable workload and poor work-life balance together accounted for another 24 percent, a warning sign that even the most committed lawyers have limits.

The qualitative responses revealed legal leaders grappling with these competing demands. Several emphasised the importance of succession planning, with one advising colleagues to "hire your successor" and build teams with complementary skills. Others stressed the need for flexible work arrangements, competitive compensation, and a culture of continuous learning. Without these fundamentals, even the most sophisticated technology strategy will falter.

For many departments, cross-border expansion adds another layer of complexity. Supporting business growth across borders emerged as the top priority for the next 12 months, chosen by 38 percent of respondents. Yet approaches to managing cross-border risk vary widely: 32 percent use dedicated teams with regional expertise, 27 percent respond ad hoc, 26 percent rely mainly on external counsel, and just 15 percent have centralised teams with tools and policies.

The pain points are telling. High legal spend across multiple firms topped the list at 30 percent, followed by inconsistent service quality between jurisdictions at 28 percent. These challenges help explain why nearly half of respondents said they would consider international legal networks as an alternative to global law firms if the value proposition is strong. Another 25 percent said they might use both models, and 15 percent are actively exploring networks.

Cooke sees this openness as a watershed moment. "The credibility of the network model depends on demonstrating, consistently, that collaboration is not just possible but embedded in the way we work. That quality is not assumed, but assured through rigorous appraisal," he notes. The survey data suggests that in-house counsel are pragmatic about service models, prioritising cost efficiency, flexible pricing, sector-specific expertise, and the ability to coordinate cross-border matters seamlessly.

Several respondents described positive changes from process improvements and strategic partnerships. Some adopted proactive risk-management frameworks, working closely with business units to identify and mitigate legal risks early. Others simplified reporting lines or centralised the global legal function, enabling faster decision-making. The introduction of contract management platforms, document automation, and AI-assisted drafting tools was frequently cited as a way to free lawyers from routine tasks.

Yet the challenges remain acute. Beyond talent and workload, respondents mentioned coordination between lawyers, quality control of deliverables, enhanced regulatory scrutiny, and the confluence of economic uncertainty and geopolitical risk. When offering guidance to peers, respondents returned to fundamentals: Be proactive, stay flexible, and invest in your people. Several urged colleagues to engage early with business units to shape strategy rather than react to crises.
