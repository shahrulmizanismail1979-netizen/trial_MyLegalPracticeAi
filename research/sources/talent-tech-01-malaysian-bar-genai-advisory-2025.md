# To Members of the Malaysian Bar and pupils in chambers

# Updates on the Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence in Legal Practice

This circular is issued further and supplemental to Circular No 342/2023, dated 24 Nov 2023, titled “The Risks and Precautions in Using Generative Artificial Intelligence in the Legal Profession, Specifically ChatGPT”. Please click here to view Circular No 342/2023.

Since November 2023, Generative AI (“GenAI”) has rapidly evolved and expanded beyond novelty to become an important tool in professional settings, including the legal profession. GenAI systems learn patterns from large datasets to generate new content in the form of text, images, graphics, code, audio, video and/or other formats. These outputs are not mere reproductions but are dynamically created in response to prompts and instructions from users. However, there is still much room for improvement in the accuracy of the output.

In our legal profession, GenAI is now being increasingly integrated into legal research platforms, contract review systems, client engagement tools, and internal knowledge management. Its potential to improve efficiency, manage time, reduce costs, and support creativity is undeniable. However, these benefits are accompanied by serious regulatory, ethical, and professional risks, particularly relating to confidentiality, accuracy, accountability, data security, and the unauthorised practice of law. Also relevant is how advocates and solicitors use the generated output as there are numerous examples, globally, of lawyers relying on unvetted and unverified outputs from GenAI in litigation, client contracts, and other legal work.

Click here to view the document on the use of GenAI in legal practice (see page 3 onwards).

This document highlights the professional obligations and legal risks arising from the use of GenAI tools, including the potential for hallucinated content, breaches of client confidentiality, and overreliance by advocates and solicitors on unverified AI-generated legal analysis.

As there are numerous GenAI tools available, this advisory is focused primarily on the responsible use of such GenAI tools by the Members of the Malaysian Bar.

Note: Whether free or paid, all GenAI platforms carry inherent risks. Members of the Malaysian Bar are reminded that all GenAI output must always be subject to human oversight, legal judgment, and ethical consideration. The guidelines in this advisory apply broadly to GenAI tools and may be supplemented from time to time with additional guidelines.

# The Use of Generative Artificial

# Intelligence ("GenAI") in Legal Practice

**The use of GenAI by advocates and solicitors must unequivocally be guided** 
**by a clear understanding of the following:**

## (1) Recommended Uses of GenAI

GenAI tools like ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Perplexity AI, DeepSeek and others offer substantial 
productivity and drafting support when used responsibly and within their limits.  Appropriate usage, with caution, 
may include:

(a)  Assisting in the production of early drafts;
     (b) Brainstorming arguments, questions, or positions for early-stage legal research;
     (c) Creating client-facing educational content or Frequently-Asked-Questions;
     (d) Rewriting complex legal content into simplified formats;
     (e) Generating checklists, curating training materials, or producing onboarding guides for employees;
     (f) Identifying general themes or risk areas in high-volume review scenarios;
     (g)  Adapting content tone or voice eg empathetic, professional or direct; and
     (h) Ensuring consistent formatting eg headings, bullet points and summaries.

## (2) Scenarios Where GenAI Should Not Be Used

GenAI tools are not, and should not become, substitutes for law books, legislation, case law, legal databases, secure 
work systems, or trained legal minds.  Avoid using GenAI to:

## (a) Generate legal advice, opinions, or conclusions;

(b) Summarise judgments or legislation with client- or court-facing consequences;

(e) Draft contracts, pleadings, submissions, cause papers, or affidavits without full legal review;
     (f) Predict case outcomes, settlement values, or judicial behaviour;

(g) Handle regulated activities (eg, conveyancing, probate forms, trust instruments) that require compliance with       
local legal requirements;

(i) Disclose or share information or documents that are subject to solicitor-client confidentiality or legal    
professional privilege.

## (3) Appropriate Stages When GenAI May Be Used

(a) At the early stages of the workflow process: drafting, ideation, or structuring output;
     (b) To accelerate tasks: curating internal training materials or ensuring consistent formatting of content;
     (c) To generate non-legal content: policy summaries or checklists; and
     (d) Executing non-sensitive tasks where confidentiality is not a concern.

## (4) Scenarios Where GenAI Should Not Be Used

(a) To provide legal advice, unless every statement is independently validated;
     (b) To finalise legal documents, without manual legal review;

## (5) Benefits of GenAI for Legal Professionals

(c) To handle personal data, privileged information, or confidential instructions; and
     (d) To handle client-specific work, where professional liability is engaged.

(6) Risks and Mitigation

| Item | Risk | Mitigation Strategy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (1) | Hallucinated citations or fake cases and/or legislation | Always verify output against official legal databases. |
| (2) | Anthropomorphism | It is imperative that users are aware, at all times, that using GenAI tools is not to be equated with interacting with a human being. You must be critical of any output generated by GenAI tools. |
| (3) | Information disorder | Verify not only legal content but also whether all “factual” content is accurately represented. |
| (4) | Breach of confidentiality | Redact all sensitive, personal or other client or employee data before inputting into a GenAI tool. |
| (5) | Inaccurate legal analysis | Treat output as suggestions only; never as authoritative. |
| (6) | Overreliance | Train all lawyers to use GenAI tools critically and not as a replacement for proper legal research. |
| (7) | Regulatory liability | Maintain clear records of professional oversight and final review details for record and audit purposes. |
| (8) | Data privacy violations | Ensure compliance with all applicable laws, including the Personal Data Protection Act 2010. |
| (9) | Loss of legal judgment | All decision-making must be done by qualified legal professionals. |

## (7) Guidelines on When to Use GenAI

As GenAI becomes increasingly integrated into legal practice, it is essential to discern when its use is appropriate.  
The 4R Decision Framework1 offers a structured approach to evaluate the possible suitability of GenAI in legal tasks.

## (8) Key Reminders for Law Firms and Legal Practitioners

Regardless of the GenAI tool you use and whether the provider is a legal content provider or not, users are reminded 
to:

(a) Read the Terms of Use to understand whether the data you entered is retained or used to train the GenAI tool;
     (b) Avoid inputting personal data such as names, identifiers (eg NRIC number, address, contact information),   
financial data (eg bank statements, account details), or client- or employee-specific facts into any GenAI tool,   
including those that are paid subscriptions;

(c) GenAI capabilities continuously evolve, therefore users must stay informed through training, internal briefings,    
and Malaysian Bar circulars and events;

legal practitioners and staff in your firm are clear about this principle;
     (e) Law firms are encouraged to conduct regular general training for all legal practitioners and its staff on the use

of GenAI and its risks and benefits, and specific training about the GenAI tools that the firm uses;
     (f) Establish internal policies on GenAI use in your firm and ensure these are disseminated to all members of the  
firm; and

(g) Where possible, ensure use of legal profession-specific GenAI tools that have better privacy controls and
            comply with legal or regulatory requirements, bearing in mind that none of these privacy controls are
            absolute.
